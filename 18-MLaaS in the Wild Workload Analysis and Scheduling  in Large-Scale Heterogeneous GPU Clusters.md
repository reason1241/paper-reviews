# MLaaS in the Wild: Workload Analysis and Scheduling  in Large-Scale Heterogeneous GPU Clusters

Date: 2026-10-03

- [Link](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/nsdi22-paper-weng.pdf)

- Problem: Running diverse machine learning (ML) workloads in heterogeneous GPU clusters entails many challenges, including low GPU utilization and long queueing delays. However, existing publicly accessible workload traces and analyses are limited in cluster scale, workload diversity, and machine heterogeneity, making it difficult to understand these challenges and improve scheduling.
- Solution: The authors present a two-month workload trace from Alibaba’s Platform for Artificial Intelligence (PAI), report observations from the trace, and describe PAI’s ML job scheduling policies, including GPU sharing and reserving-and-packing.
- Claimed technical contributions:
  - The authors share a two-month workload trace from PAI, covering diverse training and inference workloads in a large heterogeneous GPU cluster. The data includes hardware specs and time-varying resource utilizations, alongside arrival times, completion times, and resource requests and usages for GPUs, CPUs, GPU memory, and main memory.
  - One of the observations is that GPU utilization is low. Therefore, they applied GPU sharing, which guarantees a fraction of GPU memory for each task instance. GPU computation is dynamically shared among colocated instances without guaranteeing a fixed compute share.
  - Another observation is that short-running task instances can experience long queueing delays. By using predicted durations with shortest-job-first (SJF) scheduling rather than first-in-first-out (FIFO), they reduced average task completion time in trace-driven simulations.
  - Gang scheduling (launching all instances of a task together) is required for many jobs, while GPU-type and locality requirements can make demanding tasks difficult to schedule. The authors addressed this issue through reserving-and-packing (R&P), which allocates less GPU-intensive tasks to less advanced GPUs and reserves advanced machines for GPU-intensive tasks with specific hardware and locality requirements. In simulations, this reduced queueing delays compared with load balancing.
  - They also spotted that most tasks are recurring and proposed a tree-regression method for predicting average task-instance runtime using three features: a Group tag derived by hashing task meta-information (entry scripts, command-line parameters, data sources and sinks), the task’s username, and resource requests.
  - Many low-GPU tasks spend a considerable amount of time on CPUs for data processing and simulation, leaving GPUs underutilized. The authors showed that CPU contention can slow such tasks and identified CPU-to-GPU resource mismatches and load imbalance across machine types.
- Metrics / Baselines:
  - The authors measure resource utilization and allocation, duration prediction error, average task completion time, and queueing delays.
  - Measured GPU allocation with sharing is about 50% lower on average, with savings up to 73% at the peak hour, compared with a simulated no-sharing baseline (Figure 8).
  - For tasks recurring at least five times, they compare predictors using different feature sets. Group, User, and Resource (or GPU) features yield absolute percentage errors below 25% for 78% of instances (Figure 12).
  - Homogeneous-GPU trace simulations compare prediction-based SJF and oracle SJF with FIFO. The tested SJF configurations reduce average task completion time by 63–77% relative to FIFO (Figure 13).
  - In simulations with 100,000 tasks and over 500,000 gang-scheduled instances, reserving-and-packing reduces average task queueing delay by 45% overall and 68% for V100 tasks compared with load balancing (Figure 19).
- Key insight and enabling idea: Many task instances request more GPU resources than they use and spend considerable time on CPU processing or simulation, leaving GPUs underutilized and creating opportunities for GPU sharing. Recurring tasks enable runtime prediction from historical executions and task metadata, supporting SJF scheduling instead of FIFO without tracking training progress. GPU-intensive tasks have specific hardware and locality requirements, motivating reserving-and-packing. Effective scheduling therefore needs to consider workload characteristics, hardware suitability, and CPU-to-GPU resource balance.
- Novelty and impact: The authors publicly release a two-month production trace from a large MLaaS cluster with 6,742 GPUs, covering diverse training and inference workloads on heterogeneous machines. Their analysis connects fractional GPU usage, recurring tasks, hardware and locality requirements, and CPU bottlenecks to practical scheduling decisions. The trace and scheduling insights support future research and evaluation of GPU-cluster resource management under realistic workload conditions.
- Technical Qualities:
  - Framing and assumptions: The paper studies heterogeneous GPU machines running training and inference workloads. Runtime prediction relies on recurring tasks and informative historical executions. The SJF simulation assumes homogeneous GPUs and uses recorded instance runtimes.
  - Merits of the technical contributions: The publicly released trace allows other researchers to study realistic ML workloads and evaluate scheduling policies. The analysis connects workload characteristics to GPU sharing, runtime prediction, hardware-aware placement, and CPU resource requirements.
  - Does the evaluation support claims and reveal limitations of the proposed approach?: Production measurements and controlled microbenchmarks support the resource-usage and CPU-interference observations. Scheduling improvements are primarily evaluated through trace-driven simulations, so identical gains in production are not established. The paper identifies remaining load imbalance and CPU bottlenecks, while its two-month trace from one cluster limits generalization.
- Criticism:
  - Runtime prediction uses Group and historical executions, but its evaluation only includes tasks recurring at least five times. Prediction accuracy for previously unseen task groups is not established.
  - The performance model for reserving-and-packing uses the ML model, embedding size, and other task features. The paper does not clearly explain how these features are obtained or how potentially sensitive metadata is handled.
  - Many evaluation results are simulation-based, so identical scheduling gains in production are not established.
  - The reserving-and-packing policy contributes to overcrowding on less advanced GPU machines and underuse of advanced machines. CPU-to-GPU resource mismatches and load imbalance remain unresolved, and the paper does not evaluate a revised policy that addresses them.
  - The two-month trace from one production cluster may not capture longer-term workload changes or generalize to other providers and hardware configurations.
  - The GPU-sharing contention analysis relies on utilization and the number of colocated instances. It does not directly measure per-instance slowdown or establish latency isolation.
