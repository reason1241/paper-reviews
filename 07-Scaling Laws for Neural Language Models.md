# Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models

Date: 2026-09-12

- [Link](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2001.08361)

- Problem: Language model performance depends on model size, dataset size, and training compute. However, the quantitative relationships between these factors and cross-entropy loss, and how to allocate a fixed compute budget efficiently, are not well understood.
- Solution: The paper varies dataset size, model size, training compute, batch size, and training steps to establish empirical power-law relationships with cross-entropy loss and determine how to allocate a fixed compute budget efficiently.
- Claimed technical contributions:
  - The main idea of this paper is that, if we want to improve model performance with an increasing compute budget, most of the increase should go toward model size, with a smaller increase in training tokens and very little increase in the number of sequential training steps.
  - Larger models are more token-efficient than smaller models; larger models can outperform smaller models given the same number of training tokens. However, larger models require more computation for training with the same number of tokens.
  - If we want to achieve a certain level of loss, using appropriately sized larger models and training them before they converge can be more compute-efficient than training smaller models to convergence. This may not hold if we include lifetime cost (training + inference), since larger models need more computation for inference.
  - The authors proposed different equations such as (L(N)=(N_c/N)^{\alpha_N}), with (\alpha_N\approx0.076) and (N_c\approx8.8\times10^{13}); (L(D)=(D_c/D)^{\alpha_D}), with (\alpha_D\approx0.095) and (D_c\approx5.4\times10^{13}) tokens; and (L(C_{\min})=(C_c^{\min}/C_{\min})^{\alpha_C^{\min}}), with (\alpha_C^{\min}\approx0.050) and (C_c^{\min}\approx3.1\times10^8) PF-days. These indicate the relationships between loss and model size (excluding embeddings), dataset size, and optimally allocated compute when not bottlenecked by other factors. Their equations fit the experimental data well within the studied ranges.
  - Performance was strongly correlated with the number of model parameters, but model shape (e.g. the number of layers and heads, width, and depth) had a relatively small effect within the tested range when the non-embedding parameter count was held fixed.
  - The authors also proposed joint scaling equations for model size and dataset size, and for model size and training steps, to predict the effects of limited data and incomplete training on loss.
- Metrics / Baselines:
  - They measured held-out cross-entropy loss in nats per token against model size, dataset size, and estimated training compute. Across Transformer configurations, loss followed approximate power laws when other resources were sufficient, while model shape had relatively little effect at fixed non-embedding parameter count.
  - They compared Transformers with LSTMs and Universal Transformers. Transformers outperformed LSTMs by making better use of longer contexts; Universal Transformers performed slightly better per parameter but slightly worse per unit of compute.
  - They compared training curves across model sizes. Larger models reached the same loss with fewer tokens, and the compute-efficient frontier favored larger models stopped before convergence over smaller models trained near convergence.
- Key insight and enabling idea: To improve model performance with an increasing compute budget, increasing model size should be prioritized because larger models are more token-efficient than smaller models. The empirical scaling laws enable the authors to estimate how to allocate compute between model size and training duration.
- Novelty and impact: Although prior studies examined relationships between performance, dataset size, and model size, this paper provides a unified empirical framework relating cross-entropy loss to model size, dataset size, training compute, batch size, and training steps. These relationships help predict performance and guide the efficient allocation of training compute.
- Technical Qualities:
  - Framing and assumptions: There are predictable relationships between performance, dataset size, model size, and training compute, with individual power laws applying when other resources are not bottlenecks.
  - Merits of the technical contributions: Within their tested range, we can approximately predict loss given model size, dataset size, and training effort. They found that model size strongly affects performance given enough data and training, while model shape has a relatively small effect at fixed parameter count.
  - Does the evaluation support claims and reveal limitations of the proposed approach?: The experimental results fit the proposed equations well within the tested range, supporting their empirical claims. However, the authors were less confident in critical batch-size predictions outside the tested loss range, did not thoroughly investigate the small-data regime, and did not test higher learning rates specifically for short training runs.
- Criticism: They primarily tested 1,024-token contexts and models up to 1.5B non-embedding parameters, limiting evidence for longer contexts and larger models. Their compute estimate omits context-dependent attention costs, which may become significant for longer contexts. Their finding that model shape has relatively little effect does not establish that broader architectural changes are unimportant. Although the paper provides theoretical motivation, it lacks a solid theoretical explanation for the scaling laws, making their applicability beyond the tested settings uncertain.
