# Gandiva: Introspective Cluster Scheduling  for Deep Learning

Date: 2026-10-01

- [Link](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/osdi18-xiao.pdf)

- Problem: For deep learning (DL), feedback-driven exploration, which uses early results to invest more resources in promising hyperparameter-tuned models, is critical. However, existing cluster schedulers such as Kubernetes or YARN do not efficiently support that workload: they allocate GPUs exclusively until jobs finish, delaying early feedback for queued jobs. Fixed allocations can also reduce cluster efficiency because training jobs have different resource needs.
- Solution: The authors propose Gandiva, a cluster scheduling framework that utilizes the predictable mini-batch cycles of DL jobs to reduce early-feedback latency and improve efficiency in a GPU cluster.
- Claimed technical contributions:
  - The authors observed that deep learning exploration is feedback-driven, so giving multiple jobs early GPU time can be more useful than prevalent GPU-exclusive allocations that leave jobs queued.
  - They observed a cyclical pattern in DL jobs’ GPU memory usage. To exploit this characteristic, they applied introspective profiling, monitoring each job’s memory usage and mini-batch time in addition to conventional CPU and GPU usage.
  - Using this predictability, they implemented job migration, packing (running multiple jobs on a GPU simultaneously), time-slicing (allocating jobs roughly 60-second micro-tasks composed of multiple mini-batch iterations), and grow-shrink. With introspection, interfering jobs can be moved to different GPUs, while jobs that benefit from packing can be colocated.
  - They also proposed two scheduling modes: reactive and introspective. The reactive mode responds to events such as job arrivals, departures, and machine failures, placing and time-slicing jobs as needed. The introspective mode continually evaluates packing, migration, and grow-shrink decisions to improve cluster efficiency.
  - They implemented PyTorch time-slicing and optimized TensorFlow migration by modifying the frameworks.
- Metrics / Baselines:
  - The authors measure time to early feedback (100 mini-batches), GPU utilization, time to find a qualified model configuration, average job completion time (JCT), makespan, and scheduling overhead.
  - On a 180-GPU cluster with 1,000 generated jobs, Gandiva is compared with a bin-packing scheduler that does not oversubscribe GPUs. Time to early feedback falls from 2,203 to 498 seconds (77%), while average GPU utilization rises from 50.1% to 62.8% (about 26% relative). This experiment enables time-slicing and packing, but not migration or grow-shrink.
  - In hyperparameter searches on 4 and 16 P40 GPUs, Gandiva is compared with a FIFO-queue baseline. It explores almost 10× as many configurations in the tested period; when a qualified configuration appears late, it finds one 7.04× and 5.96× faster, respectively (Figures 14 and Table 3).
  - In a separate 100-GPU replay derived from a production trace, Gandiva is compared with the YARN Capacity Scheduler. Average JCT falls from 832 to 656 minutes, and makespan from 13,371 to 11,349 minutes (Table 5). The replay uses substitute models and fast-forwarding.
- Key insight and enabling idea: Feedback-driven exploration needs early results from many configurations, while DL training repeatedly executes mini-batch iterations with predictable memory cycles and measurable progress rates. Gandiva uses low-memory points to suspend jobs cheaply and uses progress measurements to refine scheduling decisions.
- Novelty and impact: Gandiva applies training-aware suspension and progress profiling to GPU-cluster scheduling, enabling time-slicing, selective packing, and migration for feedback-driven exploration. In its constructed 180-GPU workload, it reduces time to early feedback by 77% and improves GPU utilization by about 26% relative to the evaluated baseline. Its AutoML experiments also find qualified configurations faster under the tested search settings.
- Technical Qualities:
  - Framing and assumptions: Gandiva targets repetitive mini-batch training jobs in a dedicated GPU cluster. It relies on identifiable iteration boundaries, low-memory points that make suspension efficient, and measurable progress for evaluating scheduling changes. Fast migration further depends on checkpointable training state and preparing the destination in advance.
  - Merits of the technical contributions: Time-slicing gives multiple configurations early feedback, allowing an AutoML algorithm to prioritize promising ones. Continuous profiling lets Gandiva reverse harmful packing decisions and migrate jobs to improve locality and cluster efficiency.
  - Does the evaluation support claims and reveal limitations of the proposed approach?: The microbenchmarks and cluster experiments support the benefits for the tested jobs. However, the 180-GPU workload is constructed, and the 100-GPU trace replay uses substitute models and fast-forwarding. The optimized migration results use TensorFlow checkpoints up to 1.4 GB; the paper does not establish similarly low costs for much larger training state or schedules without pronounced low-memory points.
- Criticism:
  - The 180-GPU experiment runs on a physical cluster, but its 1,000 jobs are drawn from a constructed workload mix. The early-feedback and utilization gains may depend on the chosen job types, durations, and arrival pattern.
  - The 100-GPU experiment uses a synthesized workload based on a production trace, but substitutes models because the original jobs’ code and data were unavailable. Fast-forwarding was validated against a shorter full replay, yet the experiment cannot establish how Gandiva would perform with the original jobs’ resource usage and interference.
  - Optimized migration is evaluated with TensorFlow checkpoints up to 1.4 GB. The paper does not establish similarly low migration or suspension costs for much larger live training state or jobs without pronounced low-memory points; its generic PyTorch migration path is slower.
  - Gandiva provides fairness among jobs on the same server, but leaves cluster-wide fairness unaddressed. The evaluation does not examine whether lower-priority jobs can face long delays under sustained load.
  - The multi-GPU evaluation covers synchronous data parallelism, so it does not establish how well the mechanisms work for model- or pipeline-parallel training.
  - Grow-shrink is demonstrated in a microbenchmark but disabled in the cluster experiments, so its contribution to cluster-wide efficiency is not established.
